# Pitch notes (about 3 minutes)

1. **Hook (15 s).** "Every link in a network should be watched by an intrusion-detection sensor, but sensors cost money. Where do you put them so every link is covered at the lowest cost? That's minimum vertex cover, an NP-hard problem."
2. **Formulation (30 s).** One qubit per device. Cost = sensor costs + a big penalty for every unwatched link. "I checked that my Hamiltonian matches the classical cost on all 256 possible placements, and I solved it exactly by brute force so every quantum answer can be graded."
3. **The failure (30 s).** Show the left bar chart. "Standard QAOA's favorite answer is: put a sensor on everything. With a penalty, that's the easiest way to lower the average, so the optimizer learns it."
4. **The fix (30 s).** Show the right bar chart. "I changed the objective to CVaR, which only scores the best 20% of outcomes. Now the two most likely answers are optimal: 17× better than random guessing, and still 14× on a noisy model of IBM's Heron chip."
5. **Real IBM hardware (30 s).** "Then I ran it on IBM's real `ibm_fez`, the same chip my noise model copies. With IBM's error suppression on, the same story showed up on hardware: CVaR's two most common answers were both optimal placements, and standard QAOA's favorite was still 'sensors on everything.' CVaR beat standard by a wide margin. It's weaker than on the simulator, 4.5× random instead of 17×, and the noise model was about three times too optimistic."
6. **Second machine and honesty (30 s).** "I also ran it on a Rigetti chip through Amazon Braket, which has no comparable error suppression. There, noise won: CVaR's output was indistinguishable from random. So on today's hardware, error suppression is what lets CVaR's advantage survive. CVaR also beat standard QAOA on all 15 random networks I simulated, but brute force still wins at this size. No quantum advantage claimed."

## Likely judge questions

* **Why not just use brute force?** At this size you should, and I do, as ground truth. The question is how QAOA behaves as a method, which choices make it work, and how hardware noise affects it.
* **Why is the penalty so large?** It guarantees an unwatched link always costs more than covering it. A smaller penalty is valid too, but in my tests it didn't make the top answer optimal.
* **Why α = 0.2?** It's a common choice from the CVaR paper. Smaller α focuses harder on the best outcomes but makes the objective noisier to estimate.
* **Why does CVaR have lower P(valid)?** It trades average quality for a better best tail. Invalid samples are discarded classically, so only the best valid sample matters.
* **What does readout mitigation assume?** That each qubit's readout errors are independent. It's measured with two calibration circuits on the same physical qubits.
* **How strong is the IBM result, really?** CVaR's P(opt) was 0.053 (4.5× random, about 24 standard errors above chance) and beat standard QAOA by about 9 standard errors. But the output is still noisy: the two optimal answers led with 89 and 81 shots out of 4,000, against 70 for the next answer. So I say "its most common answers were optimal," not "it reliably finds the optimum."
* **What did error suppression do?** Dynamical decoupling and Pauli twirling raised CVaR's P(opt) from 0.035 to 0.053 (about 4 standard errors) and turned its top answer from invalid into optimal. Without it, CVaR's share of valid placements was no better than random.
* **Why two vendors?** IBM signup failed during the event, so I first ran on Rigetti through Amazon Braket, still in Qiskit (`qiskit-braket-provider`). The IBM account worked later the same day. They're different chips with different error suppression, so it's not a fair vendor comparison; it does show error suppression matters.
* **Standard QAOA beat CVaR on Rigetti. Doesn't that contradict you?** On Rigetti, yes, and the gap is real (z = 6.4), but neither method worked there: both top answers were invalid. Likely explanation, not tested: standard QAOA's broad lean toward "more sensors" survives noise, while CVaR's sharp peak on three exact answers needs precise interference. On IBM with error suppression, CVaR won.
* **How do you know the hardware runs weren't just bugs?** The all-0 and all-1 calibration circuits read back correctly on both machines (mean readout error 1.7% on IBM, 3.4% on Rigetti). The Braket script reproduces the ideal results on a local simulator, and bit order was tested with deliberately shuffled qubits. I also recomputed every hardware number from the raw counts with separate code.
* **What would you try next?** Fewer two-qubit gates (QAOA with reps = 1, or a layout that needs less routing), more shots and repeated runs to tighten the error bars, and IBM's error mitigation (not just suppression).
* **Why reps = 2?** It's enough to work on the simulator while keeping the circuit at 57 two-qubit gates. Deeper circuits add noise on hardware.
